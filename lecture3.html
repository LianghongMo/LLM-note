Infinite-Width Training Dynamics

Theory of Deep Learning · Boris Hanin · Princeton, Fall 2026

How the initialization and learning rate must scale with width for training to have an infinite-width limit. The NTK parametrization freezes the features and reduces training to kernel regression; the mean-field parametrization lets every neuron move, and gradient descent becomes a Wasserstein gradient flow of the neuron distribution.

\(\partial_t\mu_t=\eta\,\nabla_\vartheta\cdot\Big(\mu_t\,\nabla_\vartheta\frac{\delta\mathcal L}{\delta\mu_t}\Big)\)

Key ideas

  • The question is how the initialization and learning rate must scale with the width \(N\) for the limit \(N\to\infty\) to exist and stay nontrivial. One family covers both answers: \(f_\theta=\frac{1}{\gamma\sqrt N}\sum_i W^{(2)}_i\phi(W^{(1)}_i\cdot x/\sqrt D)\) trained with learning rate \(\eta\gamma^2\).

  • NTK (\(\gamma=1\)): each neuron moves only \(O(N^{-1/2})\), the kernel \(K_\theta\) freezes, and training is kernel regression, \(\mathbf r(t)=e^{-\eta K_\infty t/n}\,\mathbf r(0)\).

  • Mean field (\(\gamma=\sqrt N\)): each neuron moves \(O(1)\) and feels the others only through their empirical distribution \(\mu_t\), which follows the Wasserstein gradient flow \(\partial_t\mu_t=\eta\nabla\cdot(\mu_t\nabla\,\delta\mathcal L/\delta\mu_t)\).

  • Lazy training versus feature learning is decided by the parametrization, not by the width alone.

3.1 Setup#

Consider a two-layer neural network \[f_\theta(x) = s_2 \sum_{i=1}^{N} W_i^{(2)} \phi\!\left( s_1 W_i^{(1)}\cdot x \right),\] where \(x\in\mathbb{R}^D\) is the input, \(N\) is the width, and \[\theta_i = \left( W_i^{(1)},W_i^{(2)} \right)\] denotes the parameters associated with neuron \(i\).

The network can be viewed as a bipartite computational graph: the input is connected to \(N\) hidden neurons through \(W_i^{(1)}\), and the hidden neurons are connected to the output through \(W_i^{(2)}\).

We train the network by gradient descent, \[\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t),\] with, for example, the squared loss \[\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_\theta(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right)^2.\]

Throughout this discussion, we first keep the number of data points \(n\) and the training time fixed, and study the limit \[N\rightarrow\infty.\]

A more difficult problem is to study joint limits in which the number of data points or the training time also grows with \(N\).

3.2 Main Question#

The main question is:

How should the initialization and learning rate scale with the width \(N\) so that the limit \(N\rightarrow\infty\) exists and remains nontrivial?

We take \[W_{ij}^{(\ell)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),\] and introduce the unified parameterization \[\boxed{ f_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{\gamma\sqrt{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} W_i^{(2)} \phi\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} W_i^{(1)}\cdot x \right) }\] together with the learning-rate scaling \[\boxed{ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\gamma^2 \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t). }\]

Two important regimes are \[\begin{aligned} \gamma &= 1 &&\text{NTK / lazy-training regime},\\ \gamma &= \sqrt{N} &&\text{mean-field regime}.\end{aligned}\]

These two choices lead to qualitatively different infinite-width training dynamics.

3.3 NTK Regime#

Take \[\gamma=1.\] Then \[f_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} W_i^{(2)} \phi\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} W_i^{(1)}\cdot x \right).\]

The important phenomenon is that, for large \(N\), the parameters move only a small distance from their initialization. Therefore the network can be approximated by its first-order Taylor expansion around \(\theta_0\): \[\boxed{ f_\theta^{\mathrm{lin}}(x) = f_{\theta_0}(x) + \left\langle \theta-\theta_0, \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_0}(x) \right\rangle. }\]

Schematically, under suitable regularity assumptions and for finite training time, \[\begin{aligned} \sup_{t\leq T} \left\| f_{\theta_t} - f_{\theta_t}^{\mathrm{lin}} \right\| &\longrightarrow 0, \\ \sup_{t\leq T} \left\| K_{\theta_t} - K_{\theta_0} \right\| &\longrightarrow 0\end{aligned}\] as \(N\rightarrow\infty\).

Thus, in the NTK limit, training of the neural network becomes equivalent to training a linear model with the fixed feature map \[x \mapsto \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_0}(x).\]

In this sense,

At infinite width, the nonlinear neural network has linearized training dynamics around its initialization.

3.4 Derivation of NTK Dynamics#

It is convenient to consider continuous-time gradient flow, \[\frac{d\theta_t}{dt} = -\eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t).\]

Then \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} f_{\theta_t}(x) &= \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x) \cdot \frac{d\theta_t}{dt} \\ &= -\eta \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_t}(x) \cdot \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t).\end{aligned}\]

For the squared loss, \[\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_{\theta}(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right) \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x_\alpha).\]

Therefore, \[\boxed{ \frac{d}{dt} f_{\theta_t}(x) = - \frac{\eta}{n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_{\theta_t}(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right) K_{\theta_t}(x,x_\alpha), }\] where \[\boxed{ K_\theta(x,x') = \left\langle \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x), \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x') \right\rangle }\] is the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK).

For the training data, define \[f_\alpha(t)=f_{\theta_t}(x_\alpha), \qquad r_\alpha(t)=f_\alpha(t)-y_\alpha.\] Then \[\boxed{ \dot{\mathbf r}(t) = - \frac{\eta}{n} K_{\theta_t} \mathbf r(t). }\]

If the kernel becomes constant, \[K_{\theta_t} \approx K_{\theta_0} \rightarrow K_\infty,\] then \[\boxed{ \mathbf r(t) = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\eta}{n}K_\infty t \right) \mathbf r(0). }\]

Thus the entire training dynamics is determined by a fixed kernel.

3.5 Why Does the NTK Freeze?#

Because the network is a sum over neurons, \[K_\theta(x,x') = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} K_{\theta_i}(x,x'),\] so \(K_\theta=O(1)\).

For a two-layer network, the Hessian with respect to neuron parameters has a block-diagonal structure, \[\operatorname{Hess}_\theta f = \operatorname{diag} \left[ \operatorname{Hess}_{\theta_1}f, \ldots, \operatorname{Hess}_{\theta_N}f \right].\]

In NTK parameterization, each neuron contributes only \(O(N^{-1/2})\) to the network output. Correspondingly, each individual neuron moves only \[\Delta\theta_i = O(N^{-1/2})\] over an \(O(1)\) training time.

Hence \[\theta_i(t) \approx \theta_i(0),\] and the features generated by each neuron barely change.

Therefore \[\boxed{ \frac{d}{dt}K_{\theta_t} \longrightarrow 0 \qquad \text{as } N\rightarrow\infty. }\]

The nonlinear terms in the Taylor expansion vanish, leaving only the linearized dynamics.

\[\boxed{ \text{NTK limit} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{kernel learning with nearly frozen features}. }\]

3.6 Mean-Field Regime#

Now take \[\gamma=\sqrt{N}.\] Then \[\boxed{ f_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} W_i^{(2)} \phi\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} W_i^{(1)}\cdot x \right). }\]

The learning-rate scaling becomes \[\boxed{ \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta N \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t). }\]

Unlike the NTK regime, individual neurons now move by an \(O(1)\) amount during training. Therefore the feature representation can evolve nontrivially.

Define \[\vartheta_i = \left( W_i^{(1)},W_i^{(2)} \right)\] and the empirical distribution of neurons \[\boxed{ \mu_t^N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \delta_{\vartheta_i(t)}. }\]

Then the network output can be written as \[f_{\mu_t^N}(x) = \int W^{(2)} \phi\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} W^{(1)}\cdot x \right) d\mu_t^N(W^{(1)},W^{(2)}).\]

As \[N\rightarrow\infty,\] the discrete empirical measure \(\mu_t^N\) approaches a continuous probability measure \(\mu_t\).

Thus instead of describing \(N\) individual neurons, we describe the evolution of the distribution of neurons.

3.7 Dynamics of an Individual Neuron#

Define \[\psi(\vartheta;x) = W^{(2)} \phi\!\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}} W^{(1)}\cdot x \right).\]

Then \[f_{\mu}(x) = \int \psi(\vartheta;x) \,d\mu(\vartheta).\]

The gradient of the network with respect to neuron \(i\) is \[\nabla_{\vartheta_i} f_\theta(x_\alpha) = \frac{1}{N} \nabla_{\vartheta_i} \psi(\vartheta_i;x_\alpha).\]

Because the learning rate is scaled by \(N\), \[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt} \vartheta_i(t) &= -\eta N \nabla_{\vartheta_i} \mathcal{L} \\ &= -\frac{\eta}{n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_{\mu_t}(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right) \nabla_{\vartheta_i} \psi(\vartheta_i;x_\alpha).\end{aligned}\]

Therefore, \[\boxed{ \dot{\vartheta}_i = - \eta \mathcal{M} (\vartheta_i,\mu_t). }\] with \[\mathcal{M}(\vartheta,\mu) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_\mu(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right) \nabla_\vartheta \psi(\vartheta;x_\alpha),\] which the next section identifies as \(\nabla_\vartheta\,\delta\mathcal{L}/\delta\mu\).

The important point is that neuron \(i\) does not depend directly on all other neurons separately. It depends on them only through the empirical distribution \[\mu_t.\]

This is a mean-field interaction.

3.8 Evolution of the Neuron Distribution#

The loss can now be regarded as a functional of the measure, \[\mathcal{L}[\mu] = \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_\mu(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right)^2.\]

Its functional derivative is \[\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta\mu} (\vartheta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{n} \left( f_\mu(x_\alpha)-y_\alpha \right) \psi(\vartheta;x_\alpha).\]

Therefore the particle dynamics becomes \[\boxed{ \dot{\vartheta} = - \eta \nabla_\vartheta \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}} {\delta\mu}(\vartheta). }\]

If the particles follow a velocity field \[v_t(\vartheta) = -\eta \nabla_\vartheta \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}} {\delta\mu_t}(\vartheta),\] conservation of probability gives the continuity equation \[\partial_t\mu_t + \nabla_\vartheta\cdot \left( \mu_t v_t \right) = 0.\]

Substituting \(v_t\), \[\boxed{ \partial_t\mu_t = \eta \nabla_\vartheta\cdot \left( \mu_t \nabla_\vartheta \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}} {\delta\mu_t} \right). }\]

This is a Wasserstein gradient flow of the loss functional \(\mathcal{L}[\mu]\).

Indeed, \[\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{L}[\mu_t] = - \eta \int \left\| \nabla_\vartheta \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}} {\delta\mu_t} \right\|^2 d\mu_t(\vartheta) \leq 0.\]

Thus gradient descent on a finite collection of neurons becomes, at infinite width, gradient flow of a probability distribution in parameter space.

3.9 NTK vs. Mean Field#

The two limits therefore have very different physical pictures: \[\begin{aligned} \text{NTK:}\qquad &\theta_i(t)-\theta_i(0) \rightarrow 0, \\ &K_{\theta_t} \approx K_{\theta_0}, \\ &\text{features are approximately frozen}; \\[1em] \text{Mean Field:}\qquad &\theta_i(t)-\theta_i(0) = O(1), \\ &\mu_0 \rightarrow \mu_t, \\ &\text{the neuron distribution and features evolve}.\end{aligned}\]

Hence, \[\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{NTK limit} \\[2mm] \text{fixed kernel / lazy feature learning} \end{array} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \begin{array}{c} \text{Mean-field limit} \\[2mm] \text{distributional dynamics / feature learning}. \end{array} }\]

The central conceptual change in the mean-field limit is \[\boxed{ \text{many interacting neurons} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{a probability measure evolving by optimal transport}. }\]
